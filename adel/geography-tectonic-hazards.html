Tectonic Hazards

1. What is a natural hazard?

An extreme natural event that can cause loss of life, damage property or disrupt people’s lives. A volcanic eruption can threaten people and buildings nearby.

3. What evidence supports continental drift?

Wegener proposed that the continents had once formed a supercontinent called Pangaea before drifting apart.

  • Continental shapes. The outlines of continents on opposite sides of the Atlantic appear to fit together.
  • Matching rocks and mountains. Rocks of the same age and mountain belts with matching rock types across the Atlantic suggest they formed together.
  • Matching fossils. Fossils of organisms such as Mesosaurus appear on continents separated by oceans the animals could not have crossed.
  • Ancient climates. Glacial marks near today’s equator and tropical coral fossils in cold regions suggest the land once lay at different latitudes.

4. How do convection currents move plates?

Heat from the core warms material in the lower mantle. It becomes less dense and slowly rises. Near the crust it cools, becomes denser and sinks. This circulation helps move the plates above it.

What Is Plate Tectonics? · PDF pp. 33–34, 40
5. What are the four plate margins?

Destructive · → ← Oceanic and continental plates converge. The denser oceanic plate subducts and crust is recycled. Earthquakes and volcanoes can occur.

Constructive · ← → Plates diverge. Magma rises and cools to form new crust. Shallow earthquakes and volcanic eruptions can occur.

Collision · → ← Two continental plates converge and the crust folds up into mountains. Earthquakes can occur. The lesson model shows no volcanoes.

Conservative · ↑ ↓ Plates slide past each other. Crust is neither created nor destroyed. Earthquakes can occur without volcanic activity.

What Are Plate Margins? · PDF p. 26
6. Why do earthquakes and volcanoes occur at different margins?

Destructive. The plates can lock. Stress builds until they slip and release energy as an earthquake. Subduction also leads to magma formation and volcanic eruptions.

Constructive. Plates pulling apart can fracture the shallow crust and trigger earthquakes. Rising magma can erupt and create new crust as it cools.

Collision. Converging continental plates squeeze and deform the crust. Sudden movement as stress is released can cause earthquakes.

Conservative. Friction locks the sliding plates. Stress builds until they suddenly slip, releasing energy as an earthquake.

7. What is a volcano?

An opening in Earth’s crust through which lava, gases, ash and other rock material can erupt. Molten rock is called magma underground and lava once it reaches the surface.

8. How do shield and composite volcanoes compare?
Shield
Broad and gently sloping. Runny lava spreads far, usually in gentler eruptions.
Composite
Steeper sides. Layers of ash and lava build up over repeated eruptions, which can be explosive.

9. How do composite, shield and hotspot volcanoes form?

Composite at a destructive margin. Denser oceanic crust subducts. Magma forms and rises. Pressure can build beneath the surface before an eruption. Repeated eruptions build up layers of ash and lava.

Shield at a constructive margin. The plates pull apart. Magma rises and erupts. Runny lava spreads over a wide area and cools. Repeated flows build a broad shield.

Hotspot formation · USGS supplement

Hot mantle rises and melting produces magma. Magma can erupt through the plate, building a volcano above the hotspot. As the plate moves over the relatively fixed hotspot, another volcano can form, leaving a chain such as Hawai‘i.
